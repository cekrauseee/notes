[calm, conversational] Personal projects come with a particular challenge: the person deciding what’s in scope can also expand it at any time. [measured] Without a deadline, one more improvement can easily take priority over deciding that the project is ready to go live.

[thoughtful] I recognize this pattern in my own work. [slightly weary] I can spend so much energy on a detail that barely affects the result that I have little left for finishing the rest. [slight emphasis] There’s real effort and progress, but it doesn’t always lead to something anyone gets to use.

[measured] A deadline helps me set priorities and finish. [reflective] With personal projects, I have to set those limits myself, and I can always find something else to do before publishing.

[hopeful] I thought AI would help close that gap. [slightly faster] Building became much faster, along with exploring alternatives and testing ideas. [subdued] Yet I wasn’t shipping much more often. [thoughtful] That made me look more closely at the decisions I kept putting off, even as the work of building became easier.

## When moving on becomes easy

[explaining] Working faster means discovering an idea’s strengths and limitations sooner. [measured] That helps you decide whether to keep going. [slight emphasis] It also makes it easier to drop an idea and move straight on to the next one.

[reflective] For me, that became a recurring pattern. [slightly faster] I would explore an idea, find a reason to abandon it, and already be working on something else. [measured] I could build more than before, but much of that capacity was going toward starting over.

[thoughtful] It’s hard to assess that process while you’re caught up in it. [slightly faster] There’s always something underway, something new to try, another possibility that looks more promising than the last. [subdued] It keeps you busy, even when very few projects make it out into the world.

[reflective] This was already part of how I worked. [slight emphasis] AI accelerated it. [measured] Following a new idea became easier than deciding what to do with the current one.

## When every idea has to become a product

[slightly weary] The search for ideas also began taking up too much space outside the projects themselves. [thoughtful] I would look at everyday situations and wonder what I could build around them. [slightly faster] A frustration, a conversation, an ordinary activity would quickly turn into a possible product.

[warmly] I enjoy thinking this way. [hopeful] Imagining what could exist and finding ways to build it is a large part of what draws me to programming. [reflective] The trouble started when I began expecting every idea to have the potential to become something other people would use or buy.

[measured] Before I had even tried anything, I was already deciding whether it was worth building. [slightly weary] I was looking for ideas constantly, while feeling as though I had none.

[gently] A few months ago, I began easing that pressure. [measured] Giving an idea some room before deciding what it might become. [warmly] Building out of curiosity, without needing to justify all the time and effort involved. [slightly relieved] Gradually, ideas started coming more naturally again.

[thoughtful] I also had to reconsider the pace. [warmly] Personal projects take up time I want to enjoy. [slight emphasis] When every experiment is expected to become a product, even curiosity starts to come with an obligation to deliver.

## Letting a small idea stay small

[calm, conversational] The first personal project I put online was Aviões, a plane counter. [warmly] It grew out of an inside joke between my girlfriend and me, and we still use it today.

[measured] There was a concrete reason to build it, without the pressure of finding a product to sell. [warmly] The project became part of something we already enjoyed together.

[reflective] I found it telling that, among all the ideas I had evaluated for their potential, one of the simplest was the one that actually got used. [gently] It made sense to two people, and that was enough to keep going.

[explaining] That experience has shaped how I want to approach future projects: keep the scope smaller, do less polishing before publishing, and work at a pace that leaves me interested in continuing. [gently] I still care about the details, but some improvements can wait until something is live.

[hopeful] Publishing this portfolio is part of that process. [warmly] After earlier attempts that never made it online, I want a place to bring my projects together and write about what I’ve been thinking.

[reflective] I still have unfinished projects, and I still get caught up in details. [measured] Making things easier to build hasn’t removed that difficulty. [gently hopeful] For now, accepting that an idea can stay small has helped me carry it far enough to become part of someone’s life, even if that someone is just me.
